How the app thinks

Every plant record holds the conditions it needs (climate, light, soil, water, spacing, timing) and how it grows over a season. Your growing conditions describe the spot. The app compares the two and explains every mismatch in plain words, so a color is never a mystery.

There are seven factor families: site (zone, frost, light), soil (pH, texture, drainage, fertility), planting (timing, depth, spacing), care (water, pruning, support), lifecycle and harvest, neighbors (spacing, shade, companions, shared diseases) and pests and diseases.

Grey means the plant is not in the ground that week (not planted yet, harvested, or killed by frost). Because checks run at the slider's date, a radish tucked between young tomatoes can be green: it is harvested before the tomatoes fill in. Companion plants nearby show as a good note but never change the color, because the evidence for most companion pairings is weak (see below).

Factor ranking: what matters most for the outcome

The order follows the production-ecology hierarchy used in crop science: factors that define what a plant can do, factors that limit it, and factors that reduce it. Within each tier, the order reflects how often a home garden misses on that factor and how badly. The ranking holds for a typical garden; any one factor at an extreme (a hard frost, waterlogged roots, a blight year) becomes the most important one, because growth is held back by whichever factor is scarcest (Liebig's law of the minimum).

  1. DefiningTemperature and frost fit: zone, frost dates, season length versus days to maturity. Gets it wrong and the plant dies or never ripens. Fit weight 25.
  2. DefiningLight: hours of direct sun (or lux indoors). Sets the ceiling on growth and fruiting. Weight 20.
  3. LimitingWater: amount and timing, especially at critical stages like flowering and fruit set. Weight 12.
  4. LimitingDrainage and soil air: waterlogged roots rot; the top killer of houseplants and lavender. Weight 10.
  5. LimitingNutrients and fertility: heavy feeders in poor soil yield little. Weight 8.
  6. LimitingSoil pH: controls which nutrients roots can take up; matters most at the extremes and for acid lovers like blueberries. Weight 8.
  7. LimitingSpacing and room: crowding means less light, water and nutrients per plant and more disease. Includes container size. Weight 7.
  8. ReducingPests and diseases: usually trim yield; occasionally wipe a crop out (late blight, vine borers). Weight 4.
  9. ReducingPlanting technique: soil temperature at sowing, depth, hardening off. Weight 3.
  10. ReducingCare practices: pruning, support, deadheading. Weight 3 (with pet safety).
  11. ReducingCompanion planting: the smallest and least certain effect. Shade, shared diseases and nitrogen fixing are real and are handled by the other checks; most other pairings are tradition or lore. Shown as notes only.

The weights are the app's starting hypothesis, not measured results. The Find plants tab uses them for the fit score.

Can we run a regression?

Not yet, but the app can be built to collect the data for one. A regression needs outcomes (how the plant actually did) alongside the conditions. Right now we have conditions only, with typical values from references, so any “regression” today would just restate our own assumptions.

What it would take:

  • An outcome to predict: harvest weight per plant, survival to harvest, or a 1–5 health score logged by users through the season.
  • Measured inputs: the readings from the tools below (soil moisture, soil temperature, pH, sun hours) plus the plot settings.
  • Enough records: at least a few hundred plantings per crop to estimate 8–10 factors.
  • The right model: a plain linear regression would mislead, because factors don't add up — the scarcest one caps the result, and sun, temperature and water move together. Crop scientists use boundary-line analysis for this (plot yield against each factor and read the upper edge), or mixed models with crop as a grouping factor. Tree-based models with feature-importance scores are another option once the data is large.

Suggested next step: add an “How did it do?” log to each placed plant. Once enough logs exist, the fit weights above can be replaced with fitted ones.

Tool reviews

Some factors can only be measured with a tool. Each review below says what the tool measures, which app fields it fills, how to use it well, and which models independent testers rated best. Prices change; check before buying. Links go to the testers' own write-ups.

Soil moisture meters

Measures: water in the root zone, usually on a 1–10 dial. Fills: moisture reading → compared with each plant's moisture target (evenly moist, dry top inch, dry out between waterings). Typical cost: about $10–$60.

Use it well: push the probe to root depth; most probes only sense near the tip. Read a few spots and average. Salty soil or heavily fertilized mix can read wetter than it is, so confirm with a finger now and then. For hard or clay soil pick a thick probe (about ¼ in); for deep pots pick a long one.

Soil thermometers

Measures: soil temperature at seed depth. Fills: soil temperature → compared with each plant's minimum and best germination temperature (for example beans and squash want warm soil; peas and spinach sprout in cool soil). Typical cost: about $10–$30.

Use it well: read at planting depth, at least 2–3 inches for most seeds, in the morning when soil is coldest, for a few days in a row. Long 20-in stems are for compost piles; a 5–8 in stem is right for beds.

Soil pH meters

Measures: acidity or alkalinity. Fills: soil pH → compared with each plant's pH range. Typical cost: about $10–$200.

Use it well: moisten the soil, take readings in several spots and average them, and give analog meters the waiting time their instructions call for. For a new bed, a lab soil test is the gold standard and also reports nutrients and lead; use a meter for spot checks between tests.

Tested picks (Bob Vila tested 6 meters against a lab result of pH 6.42, five times over two weeks):
